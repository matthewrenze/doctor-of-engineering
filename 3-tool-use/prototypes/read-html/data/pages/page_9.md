sophy\)"), and
reality. It studies the [categories of being](/wiki/Categories_of_being
"Categories of being") and asks what exists on the most fundamental
level.[124] Another subfield of metaphysics is [philosophical
cosmology](/wiki/Philosophical_cosmology "Philosophical cosmology"). It is
interested in the essence of the world as a whole. It asks questions including
whether the universe has a beginning and an end and whether it was created by
something else.[125]

A key topic in metaphysics concerns the question of whether reality only
consists of physical things like matter and energy. Alternative suggestions
are that mental entities (such as [souls](/wiki/Soul "Soul") and
[experiences](/wiki/Experience "Experience")) and [abstract
entities](/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete "Abstract and concrete") (such as
numbers) exist apart from physical things. Another topic in metaphysics
concerns the problem of [identity](/wiki/Identity_\(philosophy\) "Identity
\(philosophy\)"). One question is how much an entity can change while still
remaining the same entity.[126] According to one view, entities have
[essential](/wiki/Essence "Essence") and [accidental
features](/wiki/Accident_\(philosophy\) "Accident \(philosophy\)"). They can
change their accidental features but they cease to be the same entity if they
lose an essential feature.[127] A central distinction in metaphysics is
between [particulars](/wiki/Particular "Particular") and
[universals](/wiki/Universals "Universals"). Universals, like the color red,
can exist at different locations at the same time. This is not the case for
particulars including individual persons or specific objects.[128] Other
metaphysical questions are whether the past [fully
determines](/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") the present and what implications
this would have for the existence of [free will](/wiki/Free_will "Free
will").[129]

### Other major branches

See also: [List of philosophies](/wiki/List_of_philosophies "List of
philosophies")

There are many other subfields of philosophy besides its core branches. Some
of the most prominent are aesthetics, philosophy of language, philosophy of
mind, philosophy of religion, philosophy of science, and political
philosophy.[130]

[Aesthetics](/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics") in the philosophical sense is the
field that studies the nature and appreciation of [beauty](/wiki/Beauty
"Beauty") and other aesthetic properties, like [the sublime](/wiki/The_sublime
"The sublime").[131] Although it is often treated together with the
[philosophy of art](/wiki/Aesthetics#Aesthetics_and_the_philosophy_of_art
"Aesthetics"), aesthetics is a broader category that encompasses other aspects
of experience, such as natural beauty.[132] In a more general sense,
aesthetics is "critical reflection on art, culture, and [nature](/wiki/Nature
"Nature")".[133] A key question in aesthetics is whether beauty is an
objective feature of entities or a subjective aspect of experience.[134]
Aesthetic philosophers also investigate the nature of aesthetic experiences
and [judgments](/wiki/Aesthetic_judgment "Aesthetic judgment"). Further topics
include the essence of [works of art](/wiki/Works_of_art "Works of art") and
the processes involved in creating them.[135]

The [philosophy of language](/wiki/Philosophy_of_language "Philosophy of
language") studies the nature and function of [language](/wiki/Language
"Language"). It examines the concepts of
[meaning](/wiki/Meaning_\(philosophy\) "Meaning \(philosophy\)"),
[reference](/wiki/Reference "Reference"), and truth. It aims to answer
questions such as how words are related to things and how language affects
human [thought](/wiki/Thought "Thought") and understanding. It is closely
related to the disciplines of logic and linguistics.[136] The philosophy of
language rose to particular prominence in the early 20th century in [analytic
philosophy](/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy") due to the works
of [Frege](/wiki/Frege "Frege") and Russell. One of its central topics is to
understand how sentences get their meaning. There are two broad theoretical
camps: those emphasizing the formal [truth conditions](/wiki/Truth_conditions
"Truth conditions") of sentences[d] and those investigating circumstances that
determine when it is suitable to use a sentence, the latter of which is
associated with [speech act theory](/wiki/Speech_act_theory "Speech act
theory").[138]

The [philosophy of mind](/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind "Philosophy of mind")
studies the nature of mental phenomena and how they are related to the
physical world.[139] It aims to understand different types of
[conscious](/wiki/Conscious "Conscious") and
[unconscious](/wiki/Unconscious_mind "Unconscious mind") [mental
states](/wiki/Mental_states "Mental states"), like [beliefs](/wiki/Belief
"Belief"), [desires](/wiki/Desire "Desire"), [intentions](/wiki/Intention
"Intention"), [feelings](/wiki/Feeling "Feeling"), [sensations](/wiki/Sense
"Sense"), and free will.[140] An influential intuition in the philosophy of
mind is that there is a distinction between the inner experience of objects
and their existence in the external world. The [mind-body problem](/wiki/Mind-
body_problem "Mind-body problem") is the problem of explaining how these two
types of thing—mind and matter—are related. The main traditional responses are
[materialism](/wiki/Materialism "Materialism"), which assumes that matter is
more fundamental; [idealism](/wiki/Idealism "Idealism"), which assumes that
mind is more fundamental; and [dualism](/wiki/Mind%E2%80%93body_dualism
"Mind–body dualism"), which assumes that mind and matter are distinct types of
entities. In contemporary philosophy, another common view is
[functionalism](/wiki/Functionalism_\(philosophy_of_mind\) "Functionalism
\(philosophy of mind\)"), which understands mental states in terms of the
functional or causal roles they play.[141] The mind-body problem is closely
related to the [hard problem of
consciousness](/wiki/Hard_problem_of_consciousness "Hard problem of
consciousness"), which asks how the physical brain can produce [qualitatively
subjective experiences.](/wiki/Qualia "Qualia")[142]

The [philosophy of religion](/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion "Philosophy of
religion") investigates the basic concepts, assumptions, and arguments
associated with [religion](/wiki/Religion "Religion"). It critically reflects
on what religion is, how to define the [divine](/wiki/Divine "Divine"), and
whether one or more gods exist. It also includes the discussion of
[worldviews](/wiki/Worldview "Worldview") that reject religious
doctrines.[143] Further questions addressed by the philosophy of religion are:
"How are we to interpret religious language, if not literally?";[144] "Is
divine omniscience compatible with free will?";[145] and, "Are the great
variety of world religions in some way compatible in spite of their apparently
contradictory theological claims?"[146] It includes topics from nearly all
branches of philosophy.[147] It differs from [theology](/wiki/Theology
"Theology") since theological debates typically take place within one
religious tradition, whereas debates in the philosophy of religion transcend
any particular set of theological assumptions.[148]

The [philosophy of science](/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of
science") examines the fundamental concepts, assumptions, and problems
associated with science. It reflects on what science is and how to distinguish
it from [pseudoscience](/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience"). It investigates
the methods employed by scientists, how their application can result in
knowledge, and on what assumptions they are based. It also studies the purpose
and implications of science.[149] Some of its questions are "What counts as an
adequate explanation?";[150] "Is a scientific law anything more than a
description of a regularity?";[151] and "Can some special sciences be
explained entirely in the terms of a more general science?"[152] It is a vast
field that is commonly divided into the philosophy of the [natural
sciences](/wiki/Natural_sciences "Natural sciences") and the philosophy of the
[social sciences](/wiki/Social_sciences "Social sciences"), with further
subdivisions for each of the individual sciences under these headings. How
these branches are related to one another is also a question in the philosophy
of science. Many of its philosophical issues overlap with the fields of
metaphysics or epistemology.[153]

[Political philosophy](/wiki/Political_philosophy "Political philosophy") is
the philosophical inquiry into the fundamental principles and ideas governing
political systems and societies. It examines the basic concepts, assumptions,
and arguments in the field of [politics](/wiki/Politics "Politics"). It
investigates the nature and purpose of [government](/wiki/Government
"Government") and compares its different forms.[154] It further asks under
what circumstances the use of political power is
[legitimate](/wiki/Political_legitimacy "Political legitimacy"), rather than a
form of simple violence.[155] In this regard, it is concerned with the
distribution of political power, social and material goods, and [legal
rights](/wiki/Legal_rights "Legal rights").[156] Other topics are
[justice](/wiki/Justice "Justice"), [liberty](/wiki/Liberty "Liberty"),
[equality](/wiki/Social_equality "Social equality"),
[sovereignty](/wiki/Sovereignty "Sovereignty"), and
[nationalism](/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism").[157] Political philosophy
involves a general inquiry into normative matters and differs in this respect
from [political science](/wiki/Political_science "Political science"), which
aims to provide empirical descriptions of actually existing states.[158]
Political philosophy is often treated as a subfield of ethics.[159]
