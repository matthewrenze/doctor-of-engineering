# Philosophy

Philosophy ('love of wisdom' in Ancient Greek) is a systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning topics like existence, reason, knowledge, value, mind, and language. It is a rational and critical inquiry that reflects on its methods and assumptions.

Historically, many individual sciences, such as physics and psychology, formed part of philosophy but are now considered separate academic disciplines. Influential traditions include Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian, and Chinese philosophy. Western philosophy originated in Ancient Greece and covers a wide range of subfields. Arabic–Persian philosophy focuses on the relation between reason and revelation. Indian philosophy combines spiritual questions about enlightenment with explorations of reality and knowledge. Chinese philosophy emphasizes practical issues about social conduct, government, and self-cultivation.

Major branches of philosophy are epistemology, ethics, logic, and metaphysics. Epistemology studies knowledge and its acquisition. Ethics investigates moral principles and right conduct. Logic studies correct reasoning and argumentation. Metaphysics examines the general features of reality, existence, objects, and properties. Other subfields include aesthetics, philosophy of language, mind, religion, science, mathematics, history, and political philosophy. Within each branch, competing schools promote different principles and methods.

Philosophers use various methods to gain knowledge, including conceptual analysis, reliance on common sense and intuitions, thought experiments, ordinary language analysis, phenomenology, and critical questioning. Philosophy is related to many fields, including sciences, mathematics, business, law, journalism, theology, and the arts, providing interdisciplinary perspectives and ethical reflections.

## Etymology

The word philosophy comes from the Ancient Greek words φίλος (philos) 'love' and σοφία (sophia) 'wisdom'. It may have been coined by the pre-Socratic philosopher Pythagoras. The term entered English from Old French and Latin, acquiring meanings related to advanced study, deep wisdom, and the study of knowledge, reality, and existence.

Before the modern age, philosophy included most forms of rational inquiry, such as natural philosophy, which encompassed physics, chemistry, and biology. The modern sense of philosophy focuses on metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics, distinguished from empirical sciences and mathematics.

## Conceptions of philosophy

### General conception

Philosophy is characterized as rational inquiry aiming to be systematic and self-reflective. It involves careful thinking about enduring human problems, often without straightforward answers, helping to understand topics, examine life, and overcome prejudices. Socrates emphasized the importance of examining life, and Bertrand Russell noted philosophy's role in freeing people from unexamined beliefs.

### Academic definitions

Definitions of philosophy vary and are controversial. Some focus on its method (pure reasoning), others on its topics (big questions about the world). Some see philosophy as an empirical but abstract science; others emphasize its contrast with science, focusing on meaning and language clarification. Phenomenologists view philosophy as a rigorous science investigating essences. Ancient Greek and Roman philosophy saw it as a spiritual practice to develop rational capacities and improve well-being.

## History

The history of philosophy provides a systematic exposition of philosophical concepts and doctrines, focusing on rational inquiry and argumentation. Traditions include Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, Latin American, and African philosophy.

### Western

Western philosophy began in Ancient Greece with the pre-Socratics, followed by Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, who expanded topics to ethics, epistemology, metaphysics, and mind. Later movements include Epicureanism, Stoicism, Skepticism, and Neoplatonism. The medieval period focused on religious topics and Christian doctrines. The Renaissance revived ancient philosophy and humanism. The modern period emphasized reason and sensory experience, influencing the Enlightenment. The 19th century saw German idealism and Marxism. The 20th century introduced formal logic, linguistic focus, pragmatism, phenomenology, existentialism, and post-structuralism, with growth in academic philosophy and female philosophers.

### Arabic–Persian

Arabic–Persian philosophy arose in the 9th century CE, influenced by Islamic theology and ancient Greek philosophy, aiming to harmonize reason and faith. Key figures include Al-Kindi, Avicenna, and Al-Ghazali, who critiqued philosophy's limits. The classical period ended in the 12th century, with later developments like Mulla Sadra and Islamic modernism addressing tradition and modernity.

### Indian

Indian philosophy integrates reality, knowledge, and spiritual enlightenment questions. It began around 900 BCE with the Vedas, foundational to Hinduism, exploring self and ultimate reality. Non-Vedic teachings include Buddhism and Jainism. The classical period featured six orthodox Hindu schools and Advaita Vedanta, systematized by Adi Shankara, who taught monism and illusion of plurality. Ramanuja founded Vishishtadvaita Vedanta, emphasizing real individual entities and devotion. The modern period engaged with Western thought, with figures like Swami Vivekananda promoting religious pluralism.

### Chinese

Chinese philosophy focuses on practical questions of social conduct, government, and self-cultivation. The 6th century BCE saw competing schools like Confucianism, Daoism, Mohism, and Legalism. Buddhism arrived in the 1st century CE, leading to new forms. Xuanxue and Neo-Confucianism developed metaphysical ethics. The 20th century introduced Chinese Marxism and New Confucianism, integrating tradition with modern ideals.

### Other traditions

Japanese philosophy synthesized Shinto, Chinese, and Indian ideas, emphasizing active engagement with reality. Neo-Confucianism influenced the Edo period, and the Kyoto School integrated Eastern spirituality with Western philosophy. Latin American philosophy includes indigenous, colonial scholasticism, positivism, liberation philosophy, and identity exploration. African philosophy began orally, focusing on community and morality, later developing systematic discussions on ethnophilosophy, pan-Africanism, postcolonialism, and critiques of Eurocentrism.

## Core branches

Philosophical questions are grouped into branches, allowing focused inquiry and interaction.

### Epistemology

Studies knowledge, its nature, acquisition, limits, truth, belief, justification, and rationality. It distinguishes declarative knowledge (facts), practical knowledge (skills), and knowledge by acquaintance. The analysis of knowledge includes the justified true belief theory and its challenges (Gettier problem). Sources of knowledge include perception, introspection, memory, inference, and testimony. Philosophical skepticism questions knowledge claims.

### Ethics

Studies right conduct, moral evaluation of character and institutions, standards of morality, and how to live well. Branches include meta-ethics (nature of morality), normative ethics (theories of right and wrong), and applied ethics (practical application). Influential normative theories are consequentialism (e.g., utilitarianism), deontology, and virtue ethics.

### Logic

Studies correct reasoning and argument evaluation. Divided into formal logic (symbolic, deductive) and informal logic (content and context-based). Deductive arguments guarantee conclusion truth if premises are true; inductive and abductive reasoning provide probable support. Logic also studies fallacies, errors in reasoning.

### Metaphysics

Studies general features of reality: existence, objects, properties, space, time, events, causation. Includes ontology (study of being), philosophical cosmology (nature of the universe), and questions about physical vs. mental and abstract entities, identity, essential vs. accidental properties, particulars vs. universals, determinism, and free will.

### Other major branches

Include aesthetics (study of beauty and art), philosophy of language (nature and function of language, meaning, reference, truth), philosophy of mind (mental phenomena and relation to physical world, mind-body problem), philosophy of religion (concepts and arguments about religion and the divine), philosophy of science (nature, methods, and implications of science), and political philosophy (principles of political systems, justice, liberty, equality, legitimacy).

## Methods

Philosophical methods are ways to conduct inquiry and justify claims, differing from natural sciences by not relying on experimental data. Methods include conceptual analysis, common sense, ordinary language analysis, thought experiments, reflective equilibrium, pragmatism, phenomenological reduction (epoché), and methodological naturalism.

## Relation to other fields

Philosophy clarifies concepts, assumptions, and methods of other fields, providing interdisciplinary perspectives. It influences medicine (bioethics), business (ethics, corporate responsibility), law (evidence, guilt), journalism (objectivity), theology (rational evaluation of doctrines), mathematics, computer science, psychology, sociology, linguistics, education, arts, and politics. Philosophical ideas have shaped political revolutions and social movements, including feminism.
